# Location pages without doorways

Google's spam policies name doorway pages directly: many pages aimed at
specific cities that funnel to the same place, or near-identical pages that
exist to catch searches rather than to help. The scaled content abuse policy
covers the same pages made at volume, whoever or whatever wrote them. A
location page is worth publishing only when it tells a person in that place
something true that the client's other pages do not.

## Build one only when all three hold

1. **The client really serves it.** A crew goes there, the client knows the
   response time, and the project says so.
2. **There is proof from there.** Jobs done there, reviews from customers
   there, photos, permits pulled there. No proof, no page: a "places we serve"
   section on the service page is the honest alternative, and we say so in
   the plan.
3. **People search for it.** The SERP for "service + place" shows a local pack
   or local results. If Google answers it with the client's city page or a map
   alone, a new page adds nothing.

## Local substance, from the project

Every location brief has a "Local substance" section (`seo_save_brief`
refuses one without it), and the draft carries that substance in the body,
not in one paragraph at the end:

- jobs done there: how many, what kind, when (month and year), from the
  client's own records;
- what customers there said, quoted exactly as written, with the first name
  and neighbourhood the review itself shows;
- the local conditions that change the work: hard water, housing stock and
  its age, frost depth, coastal air, HOA rules, the permit office and what it
  requires;
- where the crew comes from, the drive, and the real response time;
- prices or fees that differ there, from the client's price list with its
  date.

Landmarks, history and population figures are filler unless they change the
job. If the project has nothing for a place, the brief says so and asks for
it; nobody invents a job, a review or a fact about a town.

## NAP: name, address, phone

- Exactly as on the client's Google Business Profile, character for
  character, on every page and in the schema.
- A service-area business shows a street address only where it has an office
  that customers can visit. Never an invented address, a virtual office or a
  mailbox; Business Profile rules forbid them and they cost the listing.
- A page for a place without an office says where the client serves it from:
  "We serve Fairview from our Riverton shop, about 25 minutes away."
- One main phone number in the NAP and schema. A tracking number may appear in
  the page's call button; the NAP keeps the main one.

## The truth about the service area

- `areaServed` lists only places the client really serves. A map embed only
  for a real location.
- "Local" means the crew is local. When it travels, the page says how far.
- Never a footer or paragraph listing dozens of towns. Never one page per
  suburb when one area page with real substance would serve them all.

## The similarity check

`seo_save_review` compares every location draft with its sibling location
drafts in the batch: plain text, every city name masked, five-word runs,
Jaccard share. At 0.7 or more the draft goes back to the writer whatever the
editor said. Passing 0.69 is not the goal. Write so the pages would be
different with the city names removed:

- write the local sections first, and let them shape the page;
- keep shared material (what the service is, the guarantee, the process)
  short and link to the service page for the rest instead of repeating it;
- headings that say something local, not the service name with the city
  swapped.

## How the pages hang together

A service-areas hub links every location page. Each location page links up to
the hub, out to the service pages that matter there, and to one or two
neighbouring areas. Breadcrumbs follow the same path. Titles name one place:
"Water Heater Repair in Fairview, OR | Brand"; never several towns in one
title.
