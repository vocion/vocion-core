# One claim per piece

A piece that makes three claims makes none of them well enough to be believed,
and none of them can be measured. One claim, one audience, one number.

## Why it is a rule and not a preference

**It is what makes the gate possible.** The editor checks the claim against the
evidence the brief named. Three claims means three evidence sets, and in
practice it means two of them go unchecked.

**It is what makes the reading possible.** A brief is judged by a movement in
one number. A piece that argued three things and moved one number tells you
nothing about which argument moved it, so the verdict teaches the next brief
nothing.

**It is what makes the piece readable.** A reader leaves at the point where they
cannot tell what they are being told.

## What this does not mean

It does not mean short. A single claim can need three thousand words of
evidence, and often does.

It does not mean one deliverable. The same claim as a page, an email and a talk
is one brief with three deliverables and one measure.

It does not mean the piece may not mention anything else. Context, objections
and caveats all serve the claim. The test is whether a section could be removed
without weakening the claim: if it could, it belongs to another brief.

## The check, at three points

**At briefing:** if the claim cannot be written in one sentence, it is not one
claim. Split it and rank the halves separately.

**In production:** every section serves the claim, or it is filed as a note for
the strategist rather than written in.

**At the gate:** a deliverable that lands a different claim from the brief's is
`revise`, even when the different claim is better. A better claim is a better
brief, and it gets ranked like anything else.
