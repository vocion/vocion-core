# Producing from a brief

The brief is the whole instruction. You cannot ask it a question, so read it
the way it was written to be read: as a contract.

## What the brief fixes, and you do not

| The brief says | You do not |
|---|---|
| `claim` | land a different, better claim you thought of |
| `audience` | write for whoever you find easiest |
| `claimEvidence` | cite a source the brief did not name, without flagging it |
| `doNotClaim` | soften one of these into a hedge and use it anyway |
| `outOfScope` | cover it because it felt incomplete |
| `acceptanceContract` | decide a line does not apply |

A deliverable that is better than the brief asked for, in a direction the brief
did not ask for, has to be read again from the start and usually comes back.

## The deliverable is an artifact

Every piece lands as a core artifact — versions, preview, citation, share, all
free from the moment it exists — and the brief's `deliverables` entry points at
it by id. Never paste a finished piece into a message and call it delivered:
there would be nothing to version, nothing to cite and nothing for the gate to
open.

## Two lists, every time

**`assumptions` — what the brief did not say that you had to decide.** Every
line is either a fact for the next brief on this subject or the reason this
attempt went wrong. Producers who hide this list look better once and worse
forever.

**What you could not establish.** The claim you were asked to support and could
not, the figure with no source, the link that does not say what it was cited
for. Say it plainly and leave the gap.

> Less evidence produces a **smaller** deliverable, not a longer explanation of
> why the evidence is missing.

A 400-word piece that is entirely true beats a 2,000-word piece with three
sentences nobody can check — and the gate will find those three.

## Citing where the claim is made

Put the citation where the claim is, so a reader checks it in one move instead
of trusting you. Anything dated shows its date. A figure whose source you read
today and a figure you remember are different things, and only the first belongs
in the piece.

## When the brief is wrong

Sometimes the brief asks for something its own evidence cannot support. Do not
fill the gap and do not quietly narrow the claim. Produce what the evidence
supports, and say in one line which acceptance criterion cannot be met and what
would be needed to meet it. That goes back to the strategist as a better brief,
not to the reader as a hedge.
